\begin{abstract}
We study a minimal control problem from robust composite-pulse synthesis: a target
single-qubit $Z$ rotation must be built from an imperfect $Z$ evolution, whose angle carries a common
unknown multiplicative error, together with exact, instantaneous, free $X$ rotations. Our
main results are \emph{explicit} lower bounds, valid at every order $N$, on the total $Z$-evolution
cost $T$. The first is elementary: for every $0<\phi\le\pi$,
$T\ge2\,[\,2\,(2N+2)!\,\sin^{2}(\phi/4)\,]^{1/(2N+2)}$,
a coefficient $4/e\approx1.4715$ in $N$. It uses nothing beyond the factorial and a $q$-th root, and
it is pointwise stronger than the closed form $4(N+1)/e$; its proof is a doubling of flatness --
order-$N$ matrix robustness forces the scalar error carrier $h(\lambda)=1-\tfrac12\Ree\Tr(U_1^\dagger
U_\lambda)$ to vanish to order $q=2N+2$ -- followed by a single Taylor--Lagrange step at the
endpoint. The second bound refines the coefficient to $4$, at the price of an explicit threshold and
of one logarithmic correction: for every $a\ge2$ and every order $N\ge N_0(a)$ one has
$T\ge4(N+1)[1-a(\log q/q)^{2/3}]$, which at $\phi=\pi$ is the coefficient $4$ in the limit, with
$N_0(2.3)=5010$ as a concrete instance. Taking the larger of the two at each order gives a single
lower bound valid for every $N\ge1$, and the asymptotic statement
$\liminf_{N\to\infty}T_{\min}(N,\phi)/N\ge4$ is a corollary. Both results, with all constants
explicit, are machine-checked in Lean~4 with Mathlib. Numerically, at $\phi=\pi$ a local search over
free angles yields sequences whose costs grow by about $5.2$ per order for $N\le8$, below the
equiangular cost $(2N+1)\pi$; these are upper bounds only. We close by spelling out what the bounds
rule out and where incoherent errors place the useful order.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} robust quantum control; composite pulses; systematic error;
linear lower bound; Fourier approximation; formal verification; Lean.

\section{Introduction}

Composite-pulse methods provide a general way to suppress systematic control errors by replacing a
single imperfect operation with a structured sequence whose error terms cancel order by order.
Arbitrarily high-order cancellation was established in early work on composite pulses, and the
optimal asymptotic scaling of the number of primitive pulses was later characterized for important
pulse-length-error models~\cite{Brown2004,Merrill2014,Low2014}. A separate line of work has
addressed lower bounds on the physical operation time of robust composite gates, including geometric
bounds for first-order pulse-length-error robustness~\cite{Kukita2023} and, more recently, time
lower bounds for first-order robustness under bounded control amplitude~\cite{Zeng2025,Kosut2025}.
Order-$N$ cancellation is the vanishing of the first $N$ error moments, the condition that also
underlies optimized dynamical decoupling and the filter-function description of open-loop control~\cite{Kabytayev2014,Uhrig2007,PazSilva2014}, where rigorous design bounds of a different
type are available~\cite{UhrigLidar2010,Khodjasteh2011,NgLidarPreskill2011}; Section~\ref{sec:related} returns to this comparison.

The resource model considered here is deliberately different from these standard formulations.
Suppose that one available interaction generates a $Z$ rotation but its strength is not known
exactly. A second control can rotate the system about an orthogonal $X$ axis with sufficiently high
precision that, in the idealized limit, its duration may be regarded as negligible. The natural
question is then not how many control pulses are needed, but how much of the imperfect interaction
must unavoidably be accumulated in order to cancel the common systematic error to a specified
order. This distinction matters: a lower bound on the number of primitive rotations does not
immediately give a lower bound on a time resource when some rotations are assigned zero cost.

The setting also occurs naturally in Rydberg-atom control. In a recent QSP-based proposal~\cite{Ye2026}, the interaction between two Rydberg atoms is treated as an unknown but deterministic phase input, while fast Rydberg transitions are used as interleaved controls; systematic variation of the interaction strength is then suppressed by composite control. Systematic control deviations are, independently of that proposal, among the dominant
error sources of neutral-atom two-qubit gates~\cite{Evered2023,Mohan2023}. Instead, it isolates a single-qubit mathematical core:
what is the unavoidable amount of an uncertain $Z$ evolution if exact transverse rotations are free?

The results proved here are explicit, finite-order statements: a lower bound on $T$ that holds at
every prescribed order $N$, with all constants written out. They come in two layers, and the
sharper of the two is the main theorem of the paper.

First, an \emph{elementary} bound
(Theorem~\ref{thm:elementary}): order-$N$ matrix robustness forces the carrier
$h(\lambda)=1-\tfrac12\Ree\Tr(U_1^\dagger U_\lambda)$ to vanish to order $q=2N+2$ at
$\lambda=1$, and $h$ has bounded derivatives; one Taylor--Lagrange step at the endpoint
$\lambda=0$, where the propagator degenerates to a pure $X$ rotation, then gives
$T\ge2[2\,q!\,\sin^2(\phi/4)]^{1/q}$. The coefficient of $N$ is $4/e\approx1.4715$ as
$N\to\infty$, and the bound is strictly stronger than the closed form $4(N+1)/e$ at every order.
Second, a \emph{sharpened} bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}): the entire frequency band of the
carrier is used, not just one Taylor remainder point. The vanishing derivatives become $q$
vanishing moments, into which any polynomial of degree $q-1$ may be inserted; approximating
$e^{-i\mu}$ on the whole band and smoothing with a compactly supported Fourier multiplier makes
$h(0)$ exponentially small whenever $\tau\le\rho q$ with $\rho<1$, contradicting the
order-one separation of the endpoint. Carrying the constants through this argument yields the
explicit statement $T\ge4(N+1)[1-a(\log q/q)^{2/3}]$ for every $a\ge2$ and every
$N\ge N_0(a)$, i.e.\ a coefficient approaching $4$. The two layers combine into a single bound
valid at every order (Corollary~\ref{cor:allN}), and the asymptotic coefficient
$\liminf_{N\to\infty}T_{\min}(N,\phi)/N\ge4$ is then immediate.

The control model, admissibility, achievable
costs and the carrier, together with both main theorems -- the elementary bound and the
sharpened bound with its explicit threshold -- are contained in a public Lean~4 + Mathlib
development~\cite{LeanRepo}. The paper follows the Lean development closely; Section~\ref{sec:formal} lists what is machine-checked.

\section{Control model and exact mathematical statement}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Elementary rotations and composite sequences}

Let
\begin{equation}
X(\alpha):=\exp\!\left(-i\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_x\right),
\qquad
Z(\theta):=\exp\!\left(-i\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_z\right),
\label{eq:rotations}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$ are the Pauli matrices. Throughout, $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ is the
operator norm on matrices (the largest singular value), which equals one on unitary matrices, and
all numerics reported in Section~\ref{sec:numerics} use the same norm. The $X$ rotations are assumed exact,
instantaneous, and free. The $Z$ evolution is the only costly operation, and in the presence of a
fixed unknown fractional error $\eps$ its actual propagator is
\begin{equation}
Z^{\eps}(\theta)
=\exp\!\left[-i\frac{(1+\eps)\theta}{2}\sigma_z\right].
\label{eq:imperfectZ}
\end{equation}

For $L\ge0$, choose real angles $\alpha_j$ and nonnegative $Z$ angles $\theta_j$. Following the
recursive order used in the Lean development, define
\begin{equation}
U_\lambda
= X(\alpha_1)Z(\lambda\theta_1)
  X(\alpha_2)Z(\lambda\theta_2)\cdots
  X(\alpha_L)Z(\lambda\theta_L),
\label{eq:sequence}
\end{equation}
with $U_\lambda=I$ for $L=0$. Relabeling the indices gives the equivalent convention in which
$X(\alpha_L)Z(\lambda\theta_L)\cdots X(\alpha_1)Z(\lambda\theta_1)$ is written from left to right.
Nothing in the proof depends on this notational choice.

The parameter $\lambda$ is an interpolation variable: the physical propagator at error $\eps$ is
$U_{1+\eps}$. The target is
\begin{equation}
U_1=Z(\phi),\qquad 0<\phi\le\pi.
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
The cost is the total nominal $Z$ angle,
\begin{equation}
T:=\sum_{j=1}^L\theta_j.
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
The normalization is chosen so that the nominal $Z$ rotation rate is one; restoring a physical
rate $\omega$ simply replaces $T$ by $T/\omega$ in the time interpretation.

\begin{definition}[Order-$N$ robustness]
\label{def:admissible}
A sequence is \emph{order-$N$ admissible for $\phi$} if
$\theta_j\ge0$, $U_1=Z(\phi)$, and
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{d^k U_\lambda}{d\lambda^k}\right|_{\lambda=1}=0,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,N.
\label{eq:robustness}
\end{equation}
This is exactly matrix-valued flatness at the nominal error point.
\end{definition}

Let $\mathrm{costs}(N,\phi)$ denote the set of costs of all admissible sequences and define
mathematically
\begin{equation}
T_{\min}(N,\phi):=\inf\mathrm{costs}(N,\phi),
\qquad
\cstar(\phi):=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{T_{\min}(N,\phi)}{N}.
\label{eq:tmin}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Elementary bound, valid at every order]
\label{thm:elementary}
For every $0<\phi\le\pi$ and every order-$N$ admissible sequence,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
T\;\ge\;2\,\Bigl[\,2\,(2N+2)!\,\sin^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{\phi}{4}\Bigr)\Bigr]^{1/(2N+2)} .}
\label{eq:thm-elementary}
\end{equation}
In particular, at $\phi=\pi$, $T\ge 2\,[(2N+2)!]^{1/(2N+2)}$, which exceeds $4(N+1)/e$ at every
order; the limiting coefficient of $N$ is $4/e\approx1.4715$. This is the paper's main finite-order
result: it carries no threshold, no approximation theory, and no transcendental input beyond the
factorial and a $q$-th root.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Sharpened bound, coefficient $4$]
\label{thm:main}
Write $q=2N+2$. For every $a\ge2$ there is an explicit threshold $N_0(a)$ such that every
order-$N$ admissible sequence with $N\ge N_0(a)$ and target $\phi=\pi$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
T\;\ge\;4\,(N+1)\Bigl[\,1-a\,\Bigl(\frac{\log q}{q}\Bigr)^{2/3}\Bigr] .}
\label{eq:thm-sharp}
\end{equation}
Concretely the development states the corollaries with the thresholds $N\ge5010$ (for $a=2.3$) and
$N\ge46000$ (for $a=2.2$); the exact definition of $N_0(a)$, together with
the constants that enter it, is recorded in Section~\ref{sec:formal}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[A single bound at every order]
\label{cor:allN}
Fix $a\ge2$ and let $N_0(a)$ be as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. For every $N\ge1$, every
order-$N$ admissible sequence with $\phi=\pi$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
T\;\ge\;\max\Bigl\{\;2\,\bigl[(2N+2)!\bigr]^{1/(2N+2)},\;
\mathbf 1_{N\ge N_0(a)}\cdot 4\,(N+1)\Bigl[1-a\Bigl(\frac{\log q}{q}\Bigr)^{2/3}\Bigr]\Bigr\}.
\label{eq:allN}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\cstar(\pi)\ge4$; more generally, whenever admissible sequences exist at every order
and $0<\phi\le\pi$, the same whole-band argument gives $\cstar(\phi)\ge4$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[The role of the parameter $a$]
\label{rem:a}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is a one-parameter family, and no upper bound on $a$ is needed, because a
larger $a$ is never better. For fixed $q$ the bracket $1-a(\log q/q)^{2/3}$ decreases in $a$, while
the threshold $N_0(a)$ grows: it is minimal, about $5\times10^{3}$, near $a\approx2.4$, and it grows
like $256a^{3}$ beyond. Every instance with $a>2.3$ is therefore dominated by the instance $a=2.3$
-- weaker at every order and available only from a larger $N$ -- so for each $N$ the strongest statement comes from the smallest $a$ available at that $N$, and the quoted pairs $(a,N_0(a))$ are two points on a trade-off curve. For a fixed $a$ the bracket
tends to $1$ as $q\to\infty$, so the coefficient approaches $4$ for every member of the family; the
trade-off is a finite-order effect. The family is non-trivial as long as
$a< (q/\log q)^{2/3}$, which at $q=5010$ allows $a\lesssim70$.
\end{remark}

The two layers are compared at sample orders in Table~\ref{tab:twolayers}. The sharpened term
overtakes the elementary one near $N=12$, and their ratio tends to $e$, reflecting the two limiting
coefficients $4/e$ and $4$. Neither bound is asymptotic in character: each is an explicit
inequality at the stated order, and~\eqref{eq:allN} combines them into one.

The elementary bound is proved in Section~\ref{sec:elementary}; it is the estimate that holds at
every order and needs no approximation theory. The sharpened bound is proved in
Sections~\ref{sec:wholeband}--\ref{sec:mainproof}: it is established for an arbitrary $\rho<1$ by

\section{The carrier}
\label{sec:carrier}

Set
\begin{equation}
\tauvar:=\frac{T}{2},
\qquad q:=2N+2,
\label{eq:tauq}
\end{equation}
and introduce the carrier
\begin{equation}
h(\lambda):=
1-\frac12\Ree\Tr\!\left(U_1^\dagger U_\lambda\right).
\label{eq:h}
\end{equation}
This normalization makes $h$ measure endpoint separation, expose the doubled flatness order, and admit a finite-band exponential representation.

\subsection{Finite exponential bandwidth}

Because
\[
Z(\lambda\theta)
=e^{-i\lambda\theta/2}\frac{I+\sigma_z}{2}
 +e^{+i\lambda\theta/2}\frac{I-\sigma_z}{2},
\]
expanding the product in~\eqref{eq:sequence} shows that every matrix element of $U_\lambda$ is a
finite sum of exponentials $e^{i\mu\lambda}$ with
\begin{equation}
\mu=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^L s_j\theta_j,
\qquad s_j\in\{-1,+1\}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
h(\lambda)=\sum_m \gamma_m e^{i\mu_m\lambda},
\qquad |\mu_m|\le\tauvar.
\label{eq:expsum}
\end{equation}
Thus $\tauvar=T/2$ is a genuine frequency-bandwidth bound: no component of $h$ oscillates faster
than $\tauvar$.

\subsection{Bounds on the carrier}

The relative propagator $V_\lambda:=U_1^\dagger U_\lambda$ is unitary. Therefore
$|\Tr V_\lambda/2|\le1$, and hence
\begin{equation}
0\le h(\lambda)\le2.
\label{eq:h-bounds}
\end{equation}
At the nominal point $\lambda=1$, $V_1=I$. Write locally
\[
V_\lambda=a(\lambda)I+i\bm b(\lambda)\cdot\bm\sigma,
\qquad a^2+|\bm b|^2=1,
\]
with $a(1)=1$ and $\bm b(1)=0$. Matrix flatness of $U_\lambda$ through order $N$ implies
flatness of $V_\lambda$ through order $N$, so $\bm b$ has a zero of order at least $N+1$. Since
\[
h=1-a=\frac{1-a^2}{1+a}
=\frac{|\bm b|^2}{1+a}
\]
near $\lambda=1$, the zero order doubles.

\begin{proposition}[Carrier flatness]
\label{prop:flatness}
For an order-$N$ admissible sequence,
\begin{equation}
h^{(k)}(1)=0,
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,q-1,
\qquad q=2N+2.
\label{eq:hflat}
\end{equation}
In particular, the scalar flatness is a consequence of the matrix flatness and is not an additional
assumption.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The endpoint at \texorpdfstring{$\lambda=0$}{lambda=0}}

At $\lambda=0$, all $Z$ factors become the identity, and therefore
\begin{equation}
U_0=X(A),
\qquad A:=\sum_{j=1}^L\alpha_j.
\label{eq:u0}
\end{equation}
The target endpoint is $U_1=Z(\phi)$. A direct $SU(2)$ calculation gives
\begin{equation}
\Tr\!\left[Z(\phi)^\dagger X(A)\right]
=2\cos\frac\phi2\cos\frac A2.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
h(0)=1-\cos\frac\phi2\cos\frac A2.}
\label{eq:h0exact}
\end{equation}
For $0<\phi\le\pi$, $\cos(\phi/2)\ge0$, so
\begin{equation}
h(0)\ge1-\cos\frac\phi2
=2\sin^2\frac\phi4>0.
\label{eq:h0lower}
\end{equation}
The two differ: $h(0)=1-\cos(\phi/2)$ fails in general, because the free $X$ rotations leave the arbitrary factor $\cos(A/2)$.

There is also a useful geometric interpretation. For $U,V\in SU(2)$,
\begin{equation}
\norm{U-V}^2=2-\Tr(U^\dagger V),
\end{equation}
so, in the present case,
\begin{equation}
h(0)=\frac12\norm{U_1-U_0}^2.
\label{eq:h-distance}
\end{equation}
Thus the lower bound~\eqref{eq:h0lower} says that the target is uniformly separated from the
set of endpoint propagators obtainable by turning off the costly $Z$ evolution.

\section{Two elementary bounds: \texorpdfstring{$2/e$}{2/e} and \texorpdfstring{$4/e$}{4/e}}
\label{sec:elementary}

The coefficient $4$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} comes from the whole-band argument of Section~\ref{sec:wholeband}. Linear growth is much cheaper: the $2/e$ estimate below needs only the product rule, and the $4/e$ estimate of Section 4.2 is Theorem~\ref{thm:elementary}, which is machine-checked (\path{elementary_bound}). The $2/e$ estimate is kept because it shows how little is needed for $\Omega(N)$.

\subsection{Direct propagator estimate: \texorpdfstring{$2/e$}{2/e}}

Differentiating a single $Z$ factor gives
\[
\norm{\frac{d^m}{d\lambda^m}Z(\lambda\theta_j)}
=\left(\frac{\theta_j}{2}\right)^m.
\]
Applying the product rule to the whole sequence and using the unitarity of all undifferentiated
factors gives the multinomial estimate
\begin{equation}
\norm{U_\lambda^{(k)}}
\le
\sum_{k_1+\cdots+k_L=k}
\frac{k!}{k_1!\cdots k_L!}
\prod_j\left(\frac{\theta_j}{2}\right)^{k_j}
=\tauvar^k.
\label{eq:Ukbound}
\end{equation}
Taylor's theorem about $\lambda=1$, using the first $N$ vanishing derivatives, gives
\begin{equation}
\norm{U_0-U_1}
\le\frac{\tauvar^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}.
\label{eq:taylorU}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, $U_0=X(A)$ and $U_1=Z(\phi)$, and the exact endpoint geometry gives
\begin{equation}
\norm{X(A)-Z(\phi)}
\ge2\sin\frac\phi4.
\end{equation}
Therefore every admissible sequence satisfies
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
T\ge
2\left[2(N+1)!\sin\left(\frac\phi4\right)\right]^{1/(N+1)}.}
\label{eq:2e-finite}
\end{equation}
Using Stirling's formula,
$(N+1)!^{1/(N+1)}=(N+1)e^{-1}(1+o(1))$, and the fixed factor
$[2\sin(\phi/4)]^{1/(N+1)}$ tends to one. Hence
\begin{equation}
\cstar(\phi)\ge\frac2e.
\label{eq:2e}
\end{equation}

This is already enough to rule out any sublinear robustification. In particular, the conclusion
that arbitrarily high-order cancellation must cost at least $\Omega(N)$ does not depend on the
technical machinery of the main proof.

\subsection{Using the doubled flatness: \texorpdfstring{$4/e$}{4/e}}

The improvement is immediate once the carrier is introduced. From~\eqref{eq:Ukbound} and
$|\Tr M|\le2\norm M$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
|h^{(k)}(\lambda)|\le\tauvar^k.
\label{eq:hkbound}
\end{equation}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:flatness}, the first $q-1$ derivatives of $h$ vanish at $1$, where
$q=2N+2$. Taylor's theorem therefore yields
\begin{equation}
|h(0)|\le\frac{\tauvar^q}{q!}.
\label{eq:taylorh}
\end{equation}
Combining with~\eqref{eq:h0lower} gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
T\ge
2\left[2q!\sin^2\left(\frac\phi4\right)\right]^{1/q},
\qquad q=2N+2.}
\label{eq:4e-finite}
\end{equation}
Since $(q!)^{1/q}\sim q/e$, more precisely $(q!)^{1/q}=(q/e)(1+o(1))$,
\begin{equation}
\cstar(\phi)\ge\frac4e\approx1.4715.
\label{eq:4e}
\end{equation}

The factor of two in the asymptotic coefficient comes entirely from the doubled flatness order
$q=2(N+1)$. No new physical assumption is introduced. The only information not yet exploited is
that $h$ is a band-limited finite exponential sum. The main theorem obtains a further factor
$e$ by using that whole band rather than one Taylor remainder point.

\section{The whole-band argument}
\label{sec:wholeband}

\subsection{Vanishing derivatives become vanishing moments}

Let
\[
h(\lambda)=\sum_m \gamma_m e^{i\mu_m\lambda},
\qquad |\mu_m|\le\tauvar.
\]
Differentiation gives
\[
h^{(k)}(1)=i^k\sum_m \gamma_m\mu_m^k e^{i\mu_m}.
\]
Hence Proposition~\ref{prop:flatness} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
\sum_m \gamma_m\mu_m^k e^{i\mu_m}=0,
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,q-1.}
\label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
These are the moment constraints that drive the approximation argument.

If $P$ is any polynomial of degree strictly smaller than $q$, then the moments imply
\begin{equation}
\sum_m \gamma_mP(\mu_m)e^{i\mu_m}=0.
\end{equation}
Since $h(0)=\sum_m \gamma_m$, we can therefore write
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
h(0)=\sum_m \gamma_m\left[1-P(\mu_m)e^{i\mu_m}\right].}
\label{eq:replace}
\end{equation}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\left|1-P(\mu)e^{i\mu}\right|
=\left|e^{-i\mu}-P(\mu)\right|.
\label{eq:replacement-error}
\end{equation}
Thus the endpoint value can be made small whenever $e^{-i\mu}$ can be approximated on the
entire band $[-\tauvar,\tauvar]$ by a polynomial of degree $<q$ with sufficiently small error.

Identity~\eqref{eq:replace} is a discrete sum, so $P$ must be replaced by a uniformly small approximation; the next subsection builds one by Fourier inversion.

\subsection{Smoothing by a compactly supported Fourier multiplier}

Let $P$ be a degree-$(q-1)$ approximation and define on the band
\begin{equation}
\Psi(\mu):=1-P(\mu)e^{i\mu}.
\end{equation}
Up to the unimodular factor $e^{-i\mu}$ this is the $\Psi_A$ used in Section~\ref{sec:threshold}, so $|\Psi_A|=|\Psi|$.
Choose a $C^2$ cutoff $\chi$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\chi(\mu)=1\quad(|\mu|\le\tauvar),
\qquad
\chi(\mu)=0\quad(|\mu|\ge\tauvar+B),
\label{eq:cutoff}
\end{equation}
with a fixed absolute cutoff constant $K$ such that
\begin{equation}
|\chi'(\mu)|\le\frac{K}{B},
\qquad
|\chi''(\mu)|\le\frac{K}{B^2}.
\label{eq:cutoff-deriv}
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}
\widehat\kappa(\mu):=\Psi(\mu)\chi(\mu),
\qquad
\kappa(t):=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat\kappa(\mu)e^{-i\mu t}\,d\mu.
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
Because $\widehat\kappa$ is compactly supported and $C^2$, two integrations by parts give
\begin{equation}
|\kappa(t)|
\le\min\{C_1,C_2/t^2\},
\label{eq:kappa-min}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
C_1:=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R|\widehat\kappa(\mu)|\,d\mu,
\qquad
C_2:=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R|\widehat\kappa''(\mu)|\,d\mu.
\label{eq:C12}
\end{equation}
Splitting the $t$ integral at $|t|=\sqrt{C_2/C_1}$ yields the elementary estimate
\begin{equation}
\Lone{\kappa}\le4\sqrt{C_1C_2}.
\label{eq:kappaL1}
\end{equation}
On the frequency band, $\chi=1$, so~\eqref{eq:expsum} gives
\begin{align}
\int_\R h(t)\kappa(t)\,dt
&=\sum_m \gamma_m\widehat\kappa(\mu_m)\\
&=\sum_m \gamma_m\left[1-P(\mu_m)e^{i\mu_m}\right]
=h(0).
\end{align}
Together with $\norm h_\infty\le2$, this gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
|h(0)|\le8\sqrt{C_1C_2}.}
\label{eq:h0kernel}
\end{equation}
Thus the problem has been reduced to controlling the product $C_1C_2$.

\subsection{Polynomial approximation on the frequency band}
\label{subsec:approx}

The natural polynomial comes from the Fourier representation of
$e^{-i\tauvar\cos u}$. Define
\begin{equation}
c_n(\tauvar):=
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
 e^{-inu}e^{-i\tauvar\cos u}\,du.
\label{eq:fouriercoef}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
e^{-i\tauvar\cos u}
=c_0(\tauvar)+2\sum_{n\ge1}c_n(\tauvar)T_n(\cos u),
\label{eq:cheb-series}
\end{equation}
so, on $|\mu|\le\tauvar$,
\begin{equation}
e^{-i\mu}
=c_0(\tauvar)+2\sum_{n\ge1}c_n(\tauvar)T_n\!\left(\frac\mu{\tauvar}\right).
\end{equation}
Equivalently $c_n(\tauvar)=i^{-n}J_n(\tauvar)$, so the degree at which the truncation must stop is
the Bessel turning point $n\approx\tauvar$; the formal development does not introduce Bessel
functions and works directly with the Fourier coefficients.

For any $\sigma>0$, shift the contour from $u$ to $u-i\sigma$; the integrand is entire and
$2\pi$-periodic, so the integral is unchanged. Since
\[
\cos(u-i\sigma)=\cos u\cosh\sigma+i\sin u\sinh\sigma,
\]
the two factors of the shifted integrand obey
$\lvert e^{-in(u-i\sigma)}\rvert=e^{-n\sigma}$ and
$\lvert e^{-i\tauvar\cos(u-i\sigma)}\rvert=e^{\tauvar\sin u\sinh\sigma}\le
e^{\tauvar\sinh\sigma}$, so for $n>0$
\begin{equation}
|c_n(\tauvar)|
\le\exp\!\left(\tauvar\sinh\sigma-n\sigma\right).
\label{eq:cn-decay}
\end{equation}
Truncate the series after degree $q-1$ and denote the resulting polynomial by
$P_{q-1}$. A geometric tail estimate gives an approximation error bounded by
\begin{equation}
\eta
:=\sup_{|\mu|\le\tauvar}|e^{-i\mu}-P_{q-1}(\mu)|
\le
\left(1+\frac4{1-e^{-\sigma}}\right)e^\sigma
\exp\!\left(\tauvar\sinh\sigma-\sigma q\right).
\label{eq:eta-general}
\end{equation}
The prefactor is chosen to match the formal development.

Suppose now that
\begin{equation}
\tauvar\le\rho q,
\qquad0<\rho<1.
\label{eq:belowthreshold}
\end{equation}
Choose $\sigma'>0$ so small that
\begin{equation}
\eta_0:=\sigma'-\rho\sinh\sigma'>0.
\label{eq:eta0}
\end{equation}
Such a choice exists because $\sinh\sigma'=\sigma'+O((\sigma')^3)$ and $\rho<1$. Then
\begin{equation}
\eta
\le D_0 e^{-\eta_0 q},
\qquad
D_0:=\left(1+\frac4{1-e^{-\sigma'}}\right)e^{\sigma'}.
\label{eq:eta-exp}
\end{equation}
The error therefore decays exponentially in the polynomial degree as long as the frequency
bandwidth stays below the critical ratio $\tauvar/q=1$.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\tau$}{tau}-scaled collar}
\label{subsec:collar}

The polynomial approximation is required not only on the band itself. The Fourier cutoff extends
the multiplier to the collar
$\tauvar<|\mu|\le\tauvar+B$, so derivatives of $P$ must remain controlled there. A collar width
that is fixed independently of $\tauvar$ is not uniform when $\tauvar\to0$. The formalized
solution is to choose the width relative to the band:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
B:=\frac{\eta\tauvar}{q^2}.}
\label{eq:Bscaled}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
\frac{B}{\tauvar}=\frac{\eta}{q^2},
\end{equation}
which is independent of the absolute scale of $\tauvar$. Both $B$ and the error $\eta$ are
dimensionless, since angles are measured in units of the nominal $Z$ rate.

The Chebyshev polynomials satisfy the differential equation
\begin{equation}
(1-u^2)T_n''(u)=uT_n'(u)-n^2T_n(u).
\label{eq:chebODE}
\end{equation}
Using this equation together with the usual collar growth bound gives, for
$|u|\le1+\beta$ with $0\le\beta\le1/2$,
\begin{equation}
|T_n''(u)|\le32n^4e^{2n\sqrt\beta}.
\label{eq:cheb2}
\end{equation}
With the choice $\beta=B/\tauvar=\eta/q^2$, and the coefficient decay~\eqref{eq:cn-decay} on
the tail $n\ge q$ of the Chebyshev series, this controls $P$, $P'$, and $P''$ uniformly on the
whole collar. Two side conditions enter and both hold for all sufficiently large $q$, because
$\eta$ decays exponentially in $q$ while $\beta=\eta/q^2$: the collar bound requires
$\beta\le1/2$, and the collar growth $e^{2n\sqrt\beta}$ must be beaten by the decay, that is
$\sigma>2\sqrt\beta$.

The powers of $q$ come from the head of the series, $n<q$, where one uses only
$\lvert c_n(\tauvar)\rvert\le 1$ --- immediate from~\eqref{eq:fouriercoef}, whose integrand has
modulus one --- and not the exponentially large prefactor of~\eqref{eq:cn-decay}; this is what
keeps the collar estimates polynomial in $q$ rather than exponential. The product rule applied to
$\widehat\kappa=\Psi\chi$ then gives bounds for $\widehat\kappa$ and $\widehat\kappa''$: on
the band $\lvert\widehat\kappa\rvert\le\eta$, and on the collar the cutoff contributes the
factors $K/B$ and $K/B^2$ of~\eqref{eq:cutoff-deriv}, while both integrals are taken over the
support, of length at most $2(\tauvar+B)$. Appendix~\ref{app:collar} records this assembly. The
resulting calculation is technical but elementary; its important feature is the scaling in $q$ and
$\eta$, not the absolute numerical constant. The formal Lean development proves the product
estimate
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
C_1C_2
\le3000(1+K)\,\eta\,q^8,}
\label{eq:kernelproduct1}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the fixed cutoff constant. Combining with~\eqref{eq:eta-exp} gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
C_1C_2
\le
3000(1+K)\left(1+\frac4{1-e^{-\sigma'}}\right)e^{\sigma'}
q^8e^{-\eta_0q}.}
\label{eq:kernelproduct2}
\end{equation}
This is the quantitative heart of the formal proof: all dependence on $\tauvar$ has disappeared
except for the hypothesis $\tauvar\le\rho q$.

\section{Proof of the main theorem}
\label{sec:mainproof}

We now assemble the preceding ingredients exactly in the order implemented by the Lean
formalization.

Fix $0<\rho<1$. Assume, for contradiction, that there are arbitrarily large orders $N$ with an
admissible sequence satisfying
\begin{equation}
\tauvar=\frac{T}{2}\le\rho(2N+2)=\rho q.
\label{eq:smalltau}
\end{equation}
Choose $\sigma'>0$ such that
$\eta_0=\sigma'-\rho\sinh\sigma'>0$. The polynomial approximation above then satisfies~\eqref{eq:eta-exp}, and the $\tau$-scaled collar estimate gives~\eqref{eq:kernelproduct2}. Hence~\eqref{eq:h0kernel} yields
\begin{equation}
|h(0)|
\le8\sqrt{
3000(1+K)\left(1+\frac4{1-e^{-\sigma'}}\right)e^{\sigma'}
q^8e^{-\eta_0q}}
\xrightarrow[q\to\infty]{}0.
\label{eq:h0tozero}
\end{equation}
In particular, for all sufficiently large $N$,
\begin{equation}
h(0)<1-\cos\frac\phi2.
\label{eq:contradiction1}
\end{equation}
But the exact endpoint identity~\eqref{eq:h0exact} implies the opposite inequality
\begin{equation}
h(0)\ge1-\cos\frac\phi2,
\label{eq:contradiction2}
\end{equation}
which is a contradiction.

We have therefore proved the key statement
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\displaystyle
\tauvar\le\rho(2N+2)
\quad\text{is impossible for all sufficiently large }N.}
\label{eq:keysmallness}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, every sufficiently high-order admissible sequence satisfies
\[
T>2\rho(2N+2)=4\rho(N+1).
\]
Taking the infimum over admissible sequences preserves the lower bound, and letting
$\rho\uparrow1$ gives
\[
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{T_{\min}(N,\phi)}{N}\ge4.
\]
This proves the mechanism behind Theorem~\ref{thm:main}; the explicit threshold follows by carrying the constants through the same argument (Section~\ref{sec:formal}).

\subsection{From the mechanism to an explicit threshold}
\label{sec:threshold}

The argument above is stated for an arbitrary $0<\rho<1$, and the polynomial factor $q^8$ in~\eqref{eq:h0tozero} is what makes the resulting threshold implicit. For the finite-order statement~\eqref{eq:thm-sharp} the same chain is used with sharper constants; only two of them matter.

First, the collar bounds on the band error can be made \emph{linear} in the approximation error
$\eta$ instead of absolute. Writing $d=2N+1$ for the truncation degree and $\Psi_A=e^{-i\mu}-P(\mu)=e^{-i\mu}\Psi$, so that $|\Psi_A|=|\Psi|$ and only the modulus is used,
the truncated Chebyshev series satisfies, on the whole collar $|\mu|\le\tauvar+B_\sharp$,
\begin{equation}
\sup|\Psi_A'|\;\le\;\frac{c_1d^2}{\tauvar}\,\eta,
\qquad
\sup|\Psi_A''|\;\le\;\frac{c_2d^4}{\tauvar^2}\,\eta,
\label{eq:sharpcollar}
\end{equation}
with explicit constants $c_1=7$, $c_2=384$. The exponents $d^{2}$ and $d^{4}$ are the correct scalings here: the tail contributes an $n^{2}$-weighted sum, so no $d$-scaling with a $d$-independent constant can hold. The collar growth $e^{2d\sqrt{B_\sharp/\tauvar}}$ is bounded by an absolute factor. Inserting~\eqref{eq:sharpcollar} into the support-aware bounds
for $C_1$ and $C_2$ of Section~\ref{sec:wholeband}, with the cutoff scale $B_\sharp=\sqrt\eta$ and $d\le q$ (this sharpened-route width is not the $B=\eta\tauvar/q^2$ of Section~\ref{subsec:collar}),
gives
\begin{equation}
C_1C_2\;\le\;C_\sharp\,q^{\beta}\eta^{2},\qquad \beta\le4 ,
\label{eq:sharpkernelproduct}
\end{equation}
with $C_\sharp$ explicit. Compared with~\eqref{eq:kernelproduct1} the power of $q$ has dropped from
$8$ to at most $4$, and the error enters quadratically.

Second, the approximation error is exponential. With $\sigma'=\operatorname{arccosh}(1/\rho)$ and
$\mu(\rho)=\sigma'-\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}$,
\begin{equation}
\eta\;\le\;D_0(\rho)\,e^{-q\mu(\rho)},\qquad
D_0(\rho)=\Bigl(1+\frac{4}{1-e^{-\sigma'}}\Bigr)e^{\sigma'} .
\label{eq:epsD0}
\end{equation}
The function $D_0(\rho)$ is the same expression as the constant $D_0$ of~\eqref{eq:eta-exp}, evaluated at $\sigma'=\operatorname{arccosh}(1/\rho)$.
The contradiction of~\eqref{eq:h0kernel} and~\eqref{eq:contradiction2} therefore holds as soon as
$D_0(\rho)e^{-q\mu(\rho)}$ is smaller than an explicit $q$-dependent threshold, and that is an
inequality in $q$ alone. For the profile
\begin{equation}
\rho(q)=1-a\Bigl(\frac{\log q}{q}\Bigr)^{2/3},\qquad a\ge2 ,
\label{eq:rhoprofile}
\end{equation}
and using $\mu(1-u)\ge\frac{47}{50}u^{3/2}$ for $0<u<1$ and $D_0(\rho)\le13(1-\rho)^{-1/2}$ together
with the explicit constant $K_0=21.1$ of the smoothing multiplier, the criterion reduces to
$q\ge N_0(a)$ with $N_0(a)$ explicit; the two instances quoted in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} are
$N\ge5010$ for $a=2.3$ and $N\ge46000$ for $a=2.2$. Section~\ref{sec:formal} records the definitions, the
constants, and the machine-checked status of every step.

\begin{remark}[Where the coefficient $4$ comes from]
The constant is transparent once the proof is compressed to one line:
\[
q=2N+2,
\qquad
\tauvar=\frac T2,
\qquad
\tauvar<q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
h(0)\longrightarrow0.
\]
The largest bandwidth ratio for which the approximation argument gives exponential suppression is
therefore $\tauvar/q<1$. Restoring $T=2\tauvar$ and $q\sim2N$ gives
$T/N\ge2\times2=4$.
The number $4$ is thus the threshold delivered by this proof mechanism. The theorem does not
claim that the optimal control problem itself has asymptotic coefficient exactly $4$.
\end{remark}

\section{Target angle and scope}
\label{sec:angle}

Both bounds are stated for $0<\phi\le\pi$; the sharpened layer is stated at $\phi=\pi$, where the endpoint separation is largest. The endpoint lower bound~\eqref{eq:h0lower} is the only
place where the target angle enters the proof, and it is strictly positive throughout this interval.

At $\phi=\pi$ the endpoint separation is maximal:
\begin{equation}
h(0)=1
\qquad\text{for every }A,
\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
because $\cos(\pi/2)=0$. Thus $Z(\pi)$ is the target that is furthest, in the relevant endpoint
metric, from the set of pure $X$ rotations.

At the other trivial endpoint, $\phi=0$, the target is $I$ and the empty sequence already realizes
it exactly. Likewise $Z(2\pi)=-I$ differs only by a global phase and can be generated by the free
rotation $X(2\pi)=-I$. Hence no positive lower bound can extend to the endpoints where the target
becomes trivial up to global phase.

\section{Numerical upper bounds for the hardest target}
\label{sec:numerics}

Corollary~\ref{cor:allN} gives $\cstar(\phi)\ge4$ and leaves the upper end of the window open. The
gap is quantitative and worth measuring, and we take $\phi=\pi$, the target realized in the Rydberg protocol~\cite{Ye2026}, where each segment of free evolution is designed to
accumulate the phase $\pi$.

Every number here is an upper bound for $T_{\min}(N,\pi)$; the status of the data is summarized at the end of the section.

For a fixed number $L$ of $Z$ segments, write the sequence in the form~\eqref{eq:sequence},
\[
U_\lambda
=X(\alpha_1)Z(\lambda\theta_1)X(\alpha_2)Z(\lambda\theta_2)\cdots X(\alpha_L)Z(\lambda\theta_L),
\]
and consider the finite-dimensional problem
\begin{equation}
T_{\min}(N,\pi)\;\le\;\min\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^L\theta_j\Big\} ,
\qquad
\theta_j\ge0,
\qquad
U_1=Z(\pi),
\qquad
\frac{d^kU_\lambda}{d\lambda^k}\Big|_{\lambda=1}=0,\quad k\le N .
\label{eq:numproblem}
\end{equation}
Every admissible angle list satisfies Definition~\ref{def:admissible}, so its cost bounds $T_{\min}(N,\pi)$ from above (not $\cstar(\pi)$, which no single order can bound). The angles $\alpha_j$ are free; fixing them all at $\pi$ is what makes the equiangular family of~\cite{Low2014} cost $T=(2N+1)\pi$.

\paragraph{How the numbers were obtained and checked.}
The minimizations used a truncated-Taylor propagator in the error variable, in which the
derivative conditions~\eqref{eq:robustness} hold exactly within the truncated model, and a
time-budget parametrization that turns~\eqref{eq:numproblem} at fixed $T$ into a feasibility problem, pushed down by an adaptive march and
warm-started by continuation in the segment number and in the order. Every solution reported in
Table~\ref{tab:num} was then re-verified from scratch, with the three conditions kept apart:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.1em,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \emph{Nominal identity.} The nominal propagator $U_1$ is rebuilt with an independent
  \path{scipy.linalg.expm} product, one factor at a time and with no Taylor algebra, and compared
  with $Z(\pi)$ up to the global sign $-I=X(2\pi)$. That sign is harmless here: a sequence
  realizing $-Z(\pi)$ realizes $Z(\pi)$ after the free outer rotation $X(2\pi)$, with the same cost
  and the same derivatives. The mismatch $\lVert U_1\mp Z(\pi)\rVert$ is at most
  $9\times10^{-10}$ for every order $N\le12$, which is the accuracy to which the angles
  themselves are determined; it is the tolerance of the search, not the round-off of double
  precision.
\item \emph{Flatness.} Write $u_k=d^kU_\lambda/d\lambda^k$ at $\lambda=1$. Since the a priori
  bound~\eqref{eq:Ukbound} is $\lVert u_k\rVert\le\tauvar^k$, the meaningful residual is the
  dimensionless ratio $\rho_N:=\max_{1\le k\le N}\lVert u_k\rVert/\tauvar^k$; it is at most
  $1.5\times10^{-10}$ in every row of Table~\ref{tab:num}. The cancellation is therefore complete
  to the same ten digits as the nominal identity, whereas for a generic sequence of the same cost
  the norms would be of order $\tauvar^k$: at $N=12$ the largest one is $1.1\times10^{3}$, against
  $\tauvar^{12}\approx2\times10^{18}$. The coefficients $[\eps^k]U$ are evaluated twice, by a truncated
  convolution in double precision and by a matrix series at $50$ significant decimal digits; the two
  agree on $\rho_N$ to better than $1\%$, the finite precision of the first being the limiting
  factor, which is why the tabulated values come from the $50$-digit run.
\item \emph{Order $N$.} The deviation from the target,
  $\delta(\eps)=\arccos\bigl(\lvert\langle U_{1+\eps},Z(\pi)\rangle\rvert/2\bigr)$ with
  $\langle U,V\rangle=\Tr(U^\dagger V)$, is evaluated on a grid of errors above the round-off
  floor. Its local logarithmic slope rises towards $N+1$ as $\eps$ decreases. It then saturates
  once $\delta$ reaches that floor: the last three slopes are $1.98,1.99,2.00$ at $N=1$,
  $8.82,8.91,7.99$ at $N=8$, and $12.25,12.51,12.68$ at $N=12$; the final digit of the last entry at
each order sits at the round-off floor of the computation and is reproduced only to within a few
units in that digit by a different linear-algebra stack. The third slope entries at $N\ge7$ sit at the round-off floor of the computation; they are platform-dependent and are not result digits. From $N=8$ on the last entries of
  the column fall short of $N+1$ for that reason, and no order is inferred from them. Where the
  slope is resolved, the cancellation is carried to order $N$ and stops there.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the numbers say.}
Write $T_{\mathrm{num}}(N,\pi)$ for the cost of the sequence found at order $N$; by construction
$T_{\min}(N,\pi)\le T_{\mathrm{num}}(N,\pi)$, and the two need not be equal. Over the range in
which the search depth is uniform, $N\le8$, those upper bounds increase by $5.2\pm0.3$ per
order,
\[
T_{\mathrm{num}}(N,\pi)\approx5.26\,N+2.81 ,
\]
with increments $5.20,5.20,5.24,5.27,5.19,5.45,5.26$ and residuals below $0.119$. The equiangular
Every ratio $T/[(2N+1)\pi]$ lies between $0.839$ and $0.926$. The comparison available at these orders is only $T/N<(2+1/N)\pi$: the coefficient $T/N$ is above $2\pi$ at
$N=1,2$ and stays below it from $N=3$ on, reaching its smallest value $5.62$ at $N=8$. The gain over the equiangular cost may come from the free angles alone: that family fixes
every free angle at $\pi$ for algebraic convenience, while~\eqref{eq:numproblem} leaves them free,
and every cost found is below $(2N+1)\pi$. How much of the gain is available in this way is a
question these numbers do not answer. The table does establish that every listed cost is strictly below $(2N+1)\pi$ and every ratio $T/N$ strictly above the coefficient $4$.

\paragraph{Status of the numbers, and what they do not claim.}
The optimization is a local search: a damped least-squares march from warm starts, with no
certificate of optimality and no search over segment numbers beyond continuation, and the residuals
it drives to zero are feasibility conditions, not a minimality condition. What
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} verifies is \emph{only} that each printed sequence is admissible: it
realizes $Z(\pi)$ and it cancels the first $N$ derivatives. Every entry of Table~\ref{tab:num} is
therefore a genuine cost of a genuine sequence, that is, an upper bound for $T_{\min}(N,\pi)$, and
nothing more. In particular:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em,topsep=0.25em]
\item The values are \emph{not} claimed to be optimal. A cheaper sequence of the same order may
  exist, and $T_{\min}(N,\pi)$ may be strictly smaller than every number listed; these numbers give no lower bound at any finite order.
\item No asymptotic claim is made. The fit $T\approx5.26N+2.81$ describes eight upper bounds with
  residuals below $0.119$; it is not an estimate of $\cstar(\pi)$, which these numbers cannot bound
  from either side. Because the quality of the search is itself $N$-dependent---the rows $N\ge9$
  come from a different seeding and are visibly looser---the increments of Table~\ref{tab:num}
  cannot be extrapolated in $N$.
\item The segment numbers $L$ are those found by the search, not the smallest possible ones, and
  the comparison with the equiangular family is made at equal order $N$ only.
\end{itemize}

Nothing in this section is used in the proofs of Theorems~\ref{thm:elementary} and~\ref{thm:main}. The one statement the data
do establish is one-sided and independent of optimality: the equiangular cost $(2N+1)\pi$ is not
optimal at any order considered, since every listed cost is strictly below it, by a factor between
$0.839$ and $0.926$. Closing the gap $4\le\cstar(\pi)\le2\pi$ requires either a construction in the free-$X$ model that beats the equiangular family, or a proof that no construction attains $4$.

\section{Relation to previous work}
\label{sec:related}

The earliest composite-pulse constructions showed that systematic pulse-length errors can be
cancelled to arbitrarily high order~\cite{Brown2004}, and~\cite{Merrill2014} reviews the vocabulary
of order-$N$ robustness and of cost accounting in that setting. Low, Yoder, and Chuang subsequently
proved that, in a standard primitive-pulse model, the number of primitive rotations required for
arbitrary accuracy has optimal linear scaling in the robustness order, with constructions
saturating that scaling~\cite{Low2014}. Their statement has the same shape as Theorem~\ref{thm:main} --- a linear lower bound in the
robustness order together with a construction that saturates it --- but the resource is the number
of primitive rotations, in a model where those rotations are themselves part of the costly control
sequence. It therefore does not determine the minimum $Z$-evolution time in the free-$X$
model, which is what Theorem~\ref{thm:main} bounds.

The identification of order-$N$ error cancellation with the vanishing of the first $N$ moments of
the error is standard in dynamical decoupling and in the filter-function description of open-loop
control: it is the defining condition of Uhrig's optimized sequences~\cite{Uhrig2007}, and it was
put on a general footing by the transfer-function analysis of~\cite{PazSilva2014}. The
composite-pulse counterpart, a single-qubit sequence analyzed order by order in its systematic
pulse-length error, is analyzed in~\cite{Kabytayev2014}. Section~\ref{sec:wholeband} uses the same identity ---
vanishing derivatives of the propagator at the nominal point are vanishing moments of the carrier
--- but the carrier here is a function of a $Z$-evolution budget rather than of a pulse train, and
it is the additional information that this carrier is band-limited, not the identity itself, that
produces the constant $4$.

Kukita, Kiya, and Kondo derived a lower bound on operation time for first-order pulse-length-error
robust composite gates using a geometric argument~\cite{Kukita2023}. The resource model there
assigns time to the rotations that change the control axis. Here those rotations are instantaneous and free, so a geometric bound on the full control path is not a bound on $T$.

Two recent works bound noise-robust control from the side of the control amplitude.
Zeng and Deng prove a universal average-case time lower bound for first-order robustness against a
single quasi-static coherent error under bounded control amplitude, together with a construction
that is robust in time $4T$ plus a constant~\cite{Zeng2025}; that overhead is fixed and
$N$-independent, whereas the coefficient studied here is linear in the robustness order. Kosut,
Lidar, and Rabitz prove a universal lower bound on the fidelity of any robustly controlled gate as a
function of the gate duration times an aggregate uncertainty~\cite{Kosut2025}. Both are statements
about first-order or order-independent robustness, and neither is sharp in the way
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is for the model of Section~\ref{sec:model}: the present proof uses the
analytic dependence on a common scale parameter $\lambda$ and remains valid for arbitrary order $N$.

Machine-checked quantum information is by now a developed subject --- quantum programs and
compilers in Coq and Isabelle/HOL~\cite{Bordg2021}, and quantum information theory in Lean~\cite{Meiburg2025} --- but
we are not aware of a formalized lower bound in quantum control. The development behind
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is an attempt in that direction, for a model small enough that the whole
proof, analytic estimates included, is checked by the kernel.

The connection to the supplied Rydberg QSP manuscript~\cite{Ye2026} --- unpublished at the time of
writing --- is complementary. That work treats an unknown interaction phase as the input to a
QSP-style composite protocol and uses fast control pulses to suppress systematic interaction
variation. The present result identifies a resource
limitation in the idealized single-axis core of that philosophy: even with perfect free transverse
controls, high-order cancellation cannot be made sublinear in the amount of uncertain evolution.

\section{The smoothing \texorpdfstring{$L^1$}{L1} estimate}
\label{app:smoothing}

The proof of~\eqref{eq:kappaL1} is included because it is the simplest analytic component of the
formalized whole-band argument. From~\eqref{eq:kappa},
\[
|\kappa(t)|\le C_1.
\]
Twice integrating by parts gives
\[
\kappa(t)=\frac{-1}{2\pi t^2}\int_\R\widehat\kappa''(\mu)e^{-i\mu t}\,d\mu,
\]
so $|\kappa(t)|\le C_2/t^2$. Put
$t_0=\sqrt{C_2/C_1}$ and integrate separately over $|t|\le t_0$ and $|t|>t_0$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Lone{\kappa}
&\le2C_1t_0+2\int_{t_0}^{\infty}\frac{C_2}{t^2}\,dt\\
&=2\sqrt{C_1C_2}+2\sqrt{C_1C_2}
=4\sqrt{C_1C_2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since $|h(t)|\le2$,
$|\int h\kappa|\le2\Lone{\kappa}$, which gives~\eqref{eq:h0kernel}.

\section{Contour-shift estimate for the approximation coefficients}
\label{app:contour}

Let
\[
f(z)=e^{-i\tauvar\cos z}e^{-inz}.
\]
The function is entire and $2\pi$-periodic. By periodicity and Cauchy's theorem, the real-line
integral defining $c_n$ can be shifted to $z=u-i\sigma$ for $\sigma>0$:
\[
c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(u-i\sigma)\,du.
\]
The shifted modulus obeys
\[
|e^{-in(u-i\sigma)}|=e^{-n\sigma},
\]
and, because $\cos(u-i\sigma)=\cos u\cosh\sigma+i\sin u\sinh\sigma$,
\[
\left|e^{-i\tauvar\cos(u-i\sigma)}\right|
=\exp\bigl(\tauvar\,\Im\cos(u-i\sigma)\bigr)
=\exp\bigl(\tauvar\sin u\sinh\sigma\bigr)
\le e^{\tauvar\sinh\sigma}.
\]
Therefore
\[
|c_n|\le e^{\tauvar\sinh\sigma-n\sigma},
\]
which is~\eqref{eq:cn-decay}. Summing the geometric tail gives the exponential form~\eqref{eq:eta-general} used in the main proof.

\section{The collar estimate behind the product bound}
\label{app:collar}

This appendix records what enters~\eqref{eq:kernelproduct1}. The constants are those of the formal
development, which proves the inequality under the side conditions $\tauvar\le\rho q$,
$\beta=B/\tauvar=\eta/q^2\le1/2$ and $\sigma>2\sqrt\beta$.

\emph{(a) Chebyshev collar bounds.} The only delicate point in differentiating the polynomial
approximation outside the exact band is the second derivative of $T_n$. Put $u=\cosh y$, so that
$T_n(\cosh y)=\cosh ny$ and $T_n'(\cosh y)=n\sinh(ny)/\sinh y$; the second derivative is
singular in this parametrization at $y=0$, and the standard identity
\[
(1-u^2)T_n''(u)=uT_n'(u)-n^2T_n(u)
\]
is what removes the singularity: at $u=1$ it gives $T_n''(1)=n^2(n^2-1)/3$. Combining the two
descriptions --- the explicit hyperbolic combination on the collar and the differential equation at
its boundary --- gives, for $|u|\le1+\beta$ with $0\le\beta\le\tfrac12$ and
$y\le\operatorname{arcosh}(1+\beta)\le\sqrt{2\beta}\,(1+O(\beta))$,
\[
|T_n(u)|,\ |T_n'(u)|,\ |T_n''(u)|\le32n^4e^{2n\sqrt\beta},
\]
the collar growth of Chebyshev polynomials and their derivatives~\cite{MasonHandscomb}.

\emph{(b) Assembly.} The two constants of~\eqref{eq:C12} are integrals over the support
$[-(\tauvar+B),\tauvar+B]$, so each is bounded by the length of the support times the supremum of
its integrand. On the band $\widehat\kappa=\Psi$ and $\lvert\Psi\rvert\le\eta$; on the collar
$\widehat\kappa=\Psi\chi$, and the product rule with~\eqref{eq:cutoff-deriv} gives
\[
\lvert\widehat\kappa''\rvert
\le\lvert\Psi''\rvert+\frac{2K}{B}\lvert\Psi'\rvert+\frac{K}{B^2}\lvert\Psi\rvert .
\]
The polynomial $P_{q-1}$ and its first two derivatives are controlled on the whole collar by (a),
with the head bound $\lvert c_n(\tauvar)\rvert\le1$ for $n<q$ and the tail~\eqref{eq:cn-decay} for $n\ge q$. Running the explicit bookkeeping with
$\beta=\eta/q^2$ and $\eta/B=q^2/\tauvar$ gives $B M_1\le9\eta q$ and
$\tauvar^2M_2\le141q^5$, where $M_1$ and $M_2$ bound $\lvert\widehat\kappa\rvert$ and
$\lvert\widehat\kappa''\rvert$ on the support; multiplying yields~\eqref{eq:kernelproduct1} and,
with~\eqref{eq:eta-exp},~\eqref{eq:kernelproduct2}. This is exactly the place where the relative
collar scaling $B/\tauvar=\eta/q^2$ is used.

\section{Formalization versus elementary exposition}
\label{app:scope}

Three distinctions are worth keeping explicit.

First, the elementary bound of Section~\ref{sec:elementary} is not merely expository: it is a
machine-checked theorem (\path{elementary_bound}, \path{elementary_bound_pi}), together
with the pointwise comparison \path{elementary_gt_four_over_e} with $4(N+1)/e$. Only the
weaker $2/e$ estimate of Section~\ref{sec:elementary} remains paper-level; it is superseded by the
doubled-flatness bound and is kept to show how little is needed for linear growth.

Second, the present proof description uses the language of Chebyshev approximation because it makes
the whole-band idea transparent. The formal development realizes the approximation in two ways: the
band error through the Fourier coefficients of $e^{-i\tau\cos u}$ and contour shifting
(\path{Approx.lean}, \path{Band.lean}), and the collar derivative bounds of~\eqref{eq:sharpcollar} through the Chebyshev tail of the same expansion (\path{SharpDeriv.lean},
\path{SharpDerivGen.lean}). Neither route relies on a separate Bernstein-ellipse theorem as a
black box.

Third, the asymptotic theorem in \path{Theorem.lean} carries the two representation-level
assumptions discussed in Section~\ref{sec:formal}, because it is phrased through Mathlib's real-valued
\path{liminf}; the underlying analytic contradiction is the unconditional statement that an
admissible sequence cannot satisfy $\tau\le\rho(2N+2)$ for all sufficiently large $N$. The
finite-order theorems of Section~\ref{sec:formal} need no such hypothesis: they are statements about
the cost of an admissible sequence and carry nothing beyond admissibility.

\section{Numerical solutions}
\label{app:sequences}

Table~\ref{tab:num} is reproduced here as explicit data. For every order $N\le12$ at $\phi=\pi$ we
list the $L$ costly angles $\theta_j$ and the $L$ free angles $\alpha_j$ in units of $\pi$, both in
the \emph{time order} of the sequence: $\alpha_j$ is the free rotation that follows the costly
segment $\theta_j$, and the first printed entries are the ones applied first. The product that was
verified is therefore
\[
U_\lambda=X(\alpha_L)Z(\lambda\theta_L)\,X(\alpha_{L-1})Z(\lambda\theta_{L-1})\cdots
X(\alpha_1)Z(\lambda\theta_1),
\]
which is~\eqref{eq:sequence} with the indices relabeled, $j\mapsto L+1-j$. Two conventions turn the raw
optimizer output into these lists, and both are exact rather than cosmetic: surplus free rotations,
where the search produced $L+1$ of them, are merged with $X(a)X(b)=X(a+b)$, and every $\alpha_j$ is
reduced to its representative in $[0,2\pi)$, which uses $X(\alpha+2\pi)=-X(\alpha)$ and therefore
changes the propagator only by the global sign that the convention $U_1=Z(\pi)$ of~\eqref{eq:target} restores. After those two steps each row has $L$ angles of each kind, as the
sequence of the paper requires.

Each printed list was re-verified from scratch in exactly this form, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:numerics}: the nominal mismatch $\lVert U_1\mp Z(\pi)\rVert$ is below
$9\times10^{-10}$ and the flatness residual $\rho_N$ of~\eqref{eq:Ukbound} is below
$1.5\times10^{-10}$, so the printed lists carry the accuracy claimed in
Table~\ref{tab:num}; the deviations from the target follow $\eps^{N+1}$ accordingly. The same
lists, with the Bloch-sphere trajectory of every sequence and a browser-side re-computation of these
numbers, are the first resource of Table~\ref{tab:resources}.
